ABSTRACT 
FENG, ZHEN. Micromechanics-based Multiscale Lattice Modeling of Fatigue Cracking in 
Hot Mixed Asphalt. (Under the direction of Y. Richard Kim and Murthy N. Guddati.) 
This dissertation presents a novel micromechanics-based lattice procedure designed to 
characterize the cracking performance of hot mix asphalt (HMA) from its constituent 
material properties. The approach essentially consists of a series of direct-lattice models 
integrated with multiscale technique. A typical direct-lattice modeling starts with a 
preprocessor that discretizes the microstructure of a specimen, which is either physically or 
virtually fabricated, into a random truss lattice. The mixture performance can be predicted 
by analyzing such lattice network using a general-purpose finite element program FEP++. 
The cracking process in HMA is simulated by successive removal of failed links representing 
microcracks. In this study, a surface energy based failure criterion is developed to trigger the 
creation and subsequent extension of microcracks. 
Due to the disparate length scales associated with microcracking and specimen size, the 
direct-lattice modeling described above would demand unrealistic computational cost for 
modeling even a laboratory scale HMA specimens. In order to make the procedure more 
practical, the multiscale approach is implemented. Essentially, multi-scale model considers 
the effect of different-sized aggregates at different length scales. Such an approach reduces 
the computational cost significantly, while capturing the mechanical phenomenon at various 
length scales. Finally, the effectiveness of the proposed multiscale lattice procedure is 
illustrated by modeling an actual indirect tensile (IDT) test on a thin cylindrical HMA 
specimen and making quantitative comparisons with experimental measurements, including 
full-strain fields measured by the digital image correlations (DIC) technique.

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 NUMERICAL MODELING OF FATIGUE CRACKING IN HOT MIXED ASPHALT
Fatigue cracking is one of the major distresses in asphalt pavements. Numerous field and 
laboratory studies on the Superpave PG binder specification clearly indicate that the fatigue 
cracking performance of hot mixed asphalt (HMA) is dependent on the interaction of the 
aggregate and asphalt binder. This fact makes it difficult to develop a reliable component 
material specification for fatigue cracking by testing only one component. However, it is 
impossible to test asphalt-aggregate mixtures for the binder specification because asphalt 
binders are normally purchased long before aggregate sources are determined for various 
projects. Although some locally representative combinations of aggregate and binder may be 
identified, the large experimental cost involved in the mixture testing makes the testing of 
HMA an improbable alternative for the binder specification. In an attempt to reduce the cost 
of understanding and characterizing the cracking performance of HMA, and to produce an 
enhanced component material specification, a micromechanics-based numerical modeling 
procedure is developed in this study. 
In many materials, HMA being no exception, the fatigue phenomenon is driven by the 
nucleation, propagation, and coalescence of microcracks. This fact indicates that a 
microlevel simulation of this cracking phenomenon may lead to an improved qualitative and 
quantitative understanding of the fatigue performance. Over the past few decades, 
micromechanical analysis has been proposed in the study of materials such as metals, soils, 

2
rocks, and concrete. Such analysis may be conducted in two different ways, detailed 
micromechanical modeling and average micromechanical modeling. 
Detailed micromechanical modeling involves the precise simulation of micromechanical 
phenomena such as stress singularity and the propagation of each and every microcrack. 
Theoretically, such research can be performed using well-established computational 
techniques such as finite element methods. Even with the impressive computational power 
that is currently available, such detailed micromechanical simulations are not feasible due to 
the need for extremely fine meshes and computationally demanding re-meshing procedures 
(1). Furthermore, considering the main objective of characterizing the fatigue behavior at a 
macroscopic level, it is unnecessary to resort to such detailed micromechanical modeling. 
Average micromechanical modeling, as the name implies, provides an efficient alternative of 
approximate micromechanical simulation to predict macromechanical behavior in an average 
sense. For example, in soils, discrete element modeling can predict macroscopic behavior by 
approximating the grains as spherical particles and modeling the interaction between them 
using a contact model (2,3). Similar discrete element modeling techniques have been 
successfully used in modeling other granular materials and rock masses (4~6). Although 
discrete particle modeling has been used for simulating HMA (7), an alternative technique 
based on lattice modeling may be more effective due to the bond-dominant nature of the 
material. Succinctly put, lattice modeling involves approximating the continuum using a 
lattice, with each element representing an intact bond that can be broken at any time to create 
a microcrack. 

3
Lattice modeling was originally proposed by Hrennikoff (8) as a regular triangular truss 
lattice to solve classical problems of elasticity. However, due to the lack of sufficient 
computational power at that time, the model remained theoretical. In the late 1980s, lattice 
models were reintroduced by theoretical physicists (9) to calculate the problem of conduction 
and to model brittle failure in disordered materials. In the early 1990s, lattice modeling was 
introduced to simulate progressive failure in concrete by Schlangen and Van Mier et al. (10 ~ 
13). Based on these studies, it appears that lattice modeling could be successful in simulating 
damage and cracking of HMA. 
The lattice methodology developed in this study is systematically built from existing lattice 
models for homogeneous elastic materials and modified further for HMA. It is extended to 
simulate the microscopic damage and subsequent macrocracking by implementing a surface 
energy based fracture law. Later, with the help of multiscale approach, the enhanced lattice 
modeling procedure becomes capable of simulating laboratory-scale HMA specimens. 
1.2 OBJECTIVES
The primary objective of the dissertation is to develop a micromechanics-based lattice 
modeling for that would eventually result in: 
o reducing the experimental costs, 
o helping in better understanding the interaction of binder and aggregates and its effects 
on fatigue cracking, and 

4
o estimating the fatigue performance starting from its component material properties. 
In its final form, as part of the future research work, it is expected that the micromechanical 
lattice model, combined with macroscopic modeling and experimental techniques, would 
lead to an economical and effective procedure for fatigue characterization of HMA, and thus 
to enhanced design methods and performance-related mix specification for flexible 
pavements. 
1.3 ORGANIZATION OF THE THESIS
The thesis is organized in 9 chapters to describe the methodology developed in this study and 
assesses its performance. 
First, a mesh generation procedure is developed to convert the microstructure of the material 
being simulated into a network of springs or truss elements in Chapter 2. The resulting lattice 
system is then analyzed by a numerical solver in the manner of matrix structural analysis as 
described in Chapter 3. In order to simulate the progressive cracking in HMA at low 
temperature, a surface energy based fracture law is employed for quasi-brittle fracture 
analysis in Chapter 4. Complementing concepts and procedures described in the previous 
chapters, a stand-alone microstructure generation method and a comprehensive postprocessor are developed and integrated to the analysis procedure in Chapter 5. Chapter 6 
contains the discussion of computational cost, leading to the conclusion that direct-lattice 
modeling would demand an unrealistic computational cost for modeling even laboratoryscale HMA specimens. To make the procedure more practical, the multiscale lattice 

5
methodology is introduced in Chapter 7 and its effectiveness is described in Chapter 8 with 
the help of numerical simulation of an indirect tensile test (IDT). Chapter 9 summarizes the 
thesis and contains some recommendations for future work.

2. LATTICE MESH GENERATION
Lattice modeling involves approximating a continuous solid by a network of discrete links. A 
lattice approach generally consists of three major steps, i.e., 1) representing the material with 
a discrete lattice network, 2) analyzing the resulting lattice mesh with a numerical tool, and 3) 
predicting the mixture performance using numerical simulation. This chapter emphasizes the 
method of discretizing the continuum solid into lattice mesh. Such method is first developed 
and tested for homogeneous materials (Section 2.1) and extended further to heterogeneous 
HMA (Section 2.2). 
2.1 LATTICE MODELING OF HOMOGENEOUS SOLID MATERIALS
The lattice representation, essentially characterized by network geometry and link properties, 
should mimic the behavior of continuum at the macro-scale. In particular, for the case of a 
homogeneous isotropic solid, the lattice should be statistically homogeneous and isotropic. 
Statistical homogeneity is easily obtained by having uniform lattice generation parameters 
over the entire structure. On the other hand, to represent an isotropic solid, close attention to 
the lattice network is required. A regular lattice network has preferred directions due to its 
geometry, resulting in undesirable macroscopic anisotropy. Although some maneuvering can 
be done to induce isotropy on the regular lattice, it appears that a more effective way of 
obtaining macroscopic isotropy is to use a random lattice network.

2.1.1 Random Lattice Generation 
As illustrated in Fig. 2.2, the generation of a lattice begins with a regular square mesh called 
the base mesh. Each cell of the base mesh contains a single node whose exact location 
(deviation from the center) is determined based on uniform probability distribution function. 
Once the nodes are generated, the lattice network is constructed by linking the nodes using 
Delaunay’s triangulation method (15,16). The basic idea of Delaunay’s triangulation is to 
take three points and check if their circumscribing circle contains no other points inside. If so, 
these three points will be connected to form a triangle. 

9
Figure 2.2 Generation of Random Truss Lattice for Homogeneous Materials, (a) Base Mesh, 
(b) Random Distributed Nodes, and (c) Lattice Network (Delaunay Triangulation) 
It should be noted that the distribution function (that controls the node position inside each 
base cell) also affects the mechanical characteristic of the lattice network. Numerical tests 
have proved that the uniform probability distribution function is the only one among a variety 
of random distributions, including 'Beta', 'Exponential', 'Gamma', 'Geometric', 'Lognormal', 
'Poisson', 'Normal', and 'Weibull' distributions, that can guarantee macroscopic isotropy. So 
far, there is no clear theoretical evidence to explain this observation. However, further study 
of this issue is beyond the range of this study, and will not be discussed here.

2.1.3 Link Type 
The lattice link can either be a truss element (spring) or a beam element (Fig. 2.3). 
Figure 2.3 Lattice Link Types: (a) Truss Elements, and (b) Beam Elements. 
Compared with truss elements, beam elements can capture the local rotations due to the fixed 
connection at the nodes. However, as long as the element (or link) size is small (with respect 
to the length scale of the local heterogeneity in the material), both truss and beam lattice will 
lead to similar results at the macroscopic level. Beam lattice has the ability to simulate 
materials with varying Poisson’s ratios, whereas the truss lattice tends to result in a Poisson’s 
ratio of approximately 0.3 (17,18). Considering the computational cost issues and the fact 
that HMA has a Poisson’s ratio of approximately 0.3 at the room temperature, the truss 
lattice is preferred over the beam lattice. It is expected that eventually beam lattice will be 
used so as to simulate the variation of Poisson’s ratio with temperature.

2.1.3 Link Properties

properties. The mechanical properties of each link, such as Young’s modulus and specific 
surface energy, are chosen based on the component material(s) that the link lies over. The 
geometrical properties, such as cross-sectional area and link length, however, are mainly 
dependent on mesh density and usually can be used to adjust the global behavior of the lattice 
network to match the actual mechanical response of the material being simulated.

2.1.4 Verification of Lattice Model 
Three aspects of the modeling must be evaluated: (1) Are the material properties independent 
of any specific realization of the random lattice (i.e. repeatability of random generation)? (2) 
Is the lattice isotropic? (3) Are the predicted macroscopic moduli close to those of the 
material simulated? These questions are answered by using three separate realizations of 
lattice models to simulate a homogeneous elastic material plate undergoing two different 
types of deformation – axial tension and shear (Fig. 2.4). The results from the linear elastic 
analysis are summarized in Table 2.2. It can be clearly seen that all three realizations 
produce results close to each other, thus verifying the repeatability of random lattice 
generation. From the table, it can also be observed that the macroscopic properties of lattice 
models match well with the actual properties of the material being simulated. As this match 
is independent of the loading configuration, it can be concluded that the lattice is statistically 
isotropic.

extend to viscoelastic materials – the links can simply be made viscoelastic by replacing the 
spring with viscous element (Appendix A). The lattice model has been successfully used to 
simulate creep of linear viscoelastic materials. It was observed that the time dependence of 
the link properties at the microscopic scale translates directly to the macroscopic scale.

2.2 MODELING OF HETEROGENEITY
Heterogeneity can be included in lattice models through two approaches, microstructurebased and statistics-based. In the first approach, a (regular) lattice mesh is projected onto the 
actual material structure and the properties of each lattice link are assigned according to the 
material that the link lies over (Fig. 2.5). 
Figure 2.5 (a) Particle Overlay - Internal Structure, (b) Projection on Top of a Regular 
Triangular Lattice, and (c) Assigning Properties to the Beams in Each Phase.

3. NUMERICAL SOLVER
After the continuum has been discretized into a random truss lattice network, the mechanical 
performance of the material being simulated is predicted by analyzing the lattice network 
within a framework of finite element method. In this study, a general-purpose finite element 
program FEP++ (20) is modified and used as numerical tool for linear deformation analysis. 
Such numerical tool can be easily extended for damage analysis by incorporating with a 
surface energy base failure criterion as described in the following chapter. This chapter 
focuses on description of the stiffness matrix formulation, the algorithm of the proposed 
static numerical solver, and a brief discussion on an alternative numerical solver based on 
dynamic relaxation method. The implementation of the static numerical solver is given in 
Appendix D.

The dynamic relaxation method is developed from the equation of motion governing 
structural response,

4. FRACTURE LAW
The lattice approach simulates damage in bond-dominant materials by the successive 
removal of failed links, representing microcracks. Thus, in addition to the parameters 
required for linear deformation analysis (cross-sectional area and link modulus), a failure 
criterion is required to trigger the creation of microcracks. Noting that the breakage of a 
failed lattice link can be viewed as the creation of a microcrack in the continuum 
representation, it is reasonable to assume that the failure criterion is related to the energy 
needed to create the associated microcrack. In this chapter, a surface energy based failure 
criterion for quasi-brittle fracture is presented (Section 4.1) and implemented into the 
numerical solver for progressive damage analysis (Section 4.2). The current study focuses on 
quasi-brittle fracture. Its extension to viscoelastic fracture will be the part of future work. A 
preliminary discussion about the viscoelastic fracture law can be found in Appendix B. 
4.1 FAILURE CRITERION FOR QUASI-BRITTLE FRACTURE
The lattice link used in this study is modeled as linear elastic-brittle, with failure occurring 
when the energy stored in the link exceeds the surface energy of the associated microcrack. 
Based on classical fracture mechanics, the required energy to create the crack is a multiple of 
the crack area and the specific surface energy associated with the broken bond (24,25). Thus, 
the link is removed when the linear elastic strain energy ( e Ui ) exceeds the multiple of the 

34
microcrack surface area (i.e., the cross-sectional area, Ai ) and the specific surface energy 
( i γ ); that is, the link breaks when

In equation (4-1), the surface energy, i γ , is actually representing the interface surface energy 
in the lattice system. For a binder-to-binder link, i γ is the surface energy of cohesion – the 
energy required to create a unit area crack within the binder, which is twice the specific 
surface energy of the asphalt binder (Γb ), 
 i = 2Γb γ (4-2)
For a binder-to-aggregate link, i γ is the surface energy of adhesion – the energy required to 
create a unit area crack between the binder and aggregate, which can be calculated using the 
following equation:

where Γa is the aggregate surface energy, and Γab is derived from the interface effects

4.2 IMPLEMENTATION OF QUASI-BRITTLE FRACTURE CRITERION
The above failure criterion is implemented into the numerical solver through two different 
ways, i.e., displacement control and damage control. 
4.2.1 Displacement Control 
Direct implementation is based on gradually increasing the displacement with time. The 
procedure is as follows: 

36
At time n t = t , 
1) Read load information from loading history 
2) Formulate global stiffness matrix K based on the contribution from the available 
links (i.e., the links that have not been removed from the system). 
3) Solve the linear system F = KU for the nodal displacements and convert the 
results into the link axial strain ε . 
4) Compare the axial strain with the critical strain cr ε (failure strain threshold), i.e., 
For link i, if it is tensile strain and i cr ε ≥ ε , remove it from the network. 
5) Increase time as n n n t = t + ∆t +1 , where n ∆t is time step length for current step. 
6) Go back to step 1) and repeat the above procedures for the analysis at next time 
step. 
At each time step, the analysis is performed in a straightforward manner. Lattice is solved as 
a linear elastic system under “static load” (instant value of loading history at this time). All 
the links with the tensile strain larger than the critical failure strain are simultaneously 
removed from the network, and are not considered in the subsequent analysis. In the above 
procedure, the time step length has to be small enough to capture the actual material behavior. 
This is especially important in the case of quasi-brittle failure, which has a very stiff postpeak part in the load-deflection curve. Large time step length will capture neither the peak 
load value nor the load-deflection curve. On the other hand, reducing the time step length 
increases the computational cost. Thus, the use of the above algorithm requires an optimized

time step length. This can be illustrated by the following example of a single edge notch

digital imaging process requires physical fabrication of the specimens, indicating that for 
each mixture to be evaluated one must fabricate several specimens, digitally image the 
specimens, and process the images so that they can be used in micromechanical simulation. 
Considering the prohibitive number of possible mixture designs, such a requirement of 
physical fabrication could be very expensive and time-consuming. In order to reduce, and 
possibly eliminate the need for the physical fabrication of specimens, an alternative to digital 
imaging, namely virtual fabrication, is developed to facilitate the numerical modeling.

6. STUDY OF COMPUTATIONAL COST 
6.1 DIRECT-LATTICE MODELING OF IDT 
Since one of the goals of lattice modeling is to relate the component material properties to the 
mixture properties, it is desirable to capture the effect of even the finest aggregates. Such 
detailed modeling, although appears straightforward at the outset, has significant practical 
limitations due to the prohibitive computational cost. It turns out that the simulation of 
commonly used HMA specimens is impractical with current computing power. For example, 
to capture the effect of all the aggregates larger than 0.3 mm, it is necessary to choose a 
lattice link length of 0.03 mm or smaller. With this requirement, it turns out that a 150 mm 
diameter IDT specimen (Fig. 6.1) would require approximately 28,000,000 nodes in the 
lattice mesh, thus making the analysis impractical.

6.2 TECHNIQUES TO REDUCE COMPUTATIONAL COST
Increasing the link length can reduce the system size, thus leading to more tractable 
computational modeling. However, because larger links cannot model the smaller aggregates, 
there is a significant loss of accuracy. In the past decade, different techniques have been 
proposed to reduce the computational cost for lattice analysis. Vervuurt et al. (33) used lattice 
mesh with different density for different area: a finer lattice used around the crack tip (or 
notch) and a coarser mesh represents the surrounding material.

These methods, as well as the well-know boundary element method, are well suited for

In this study, we first attempted to reduce the analysis cost through two approaches, i.e., 1) 
reducing the lattice system size, and 2) improving the computational efficiency. One efficient 
approach to reduce system size (the total number of degree-of-freedoms) is to represent the 
aggregate particle with its boundary nodes only. Consider the fact that for the most part, 
HMA consists of rigid aggregates that do not deform or crack at the temperature range where 
fatigue cracking is of concern. Deformation and cracking only occurs inside the binder (or 
mastic), which is a minor volumetric portion of the mixture. Therefore, it is desirable to 
confine the detailed lattice modeling to this active region. Indeed, noting that the aggregates 
cannot deform, the nodes around the aggregate are constrained with each other, and the 
lattice mesh inside the inclusion is simply removed (Fig. 6.4).

7. MULTISCALE MODELING APPROACH
Essentially, the multiscale approach considers the effect of different-sized aggregates at 
different scales. Such an approach reduces the computational cost significantly while 
capturing the mechanical phenomena at various scale lengths. The enhanced multiscale 
lattice procedure combined with virtual fabrication technique is illustrated in Fig. 7.1. 
As shown in the figure, given the mixture aggregate gradation, inverse stereology is applied 
to obtain the aggregate gradation in terms of the apparent aggregate area percentage. Then, 
the resulting 2D apparent aggregate gradation may be divided into a series of sub-regions 
based on different scales of observation (i.e., aggregate size). 
The lattice modeling starts with the virtual fabrication of a group of representative volume 
elements (RVEs) at the last (smallest) scale, scale n. Noting that the heterogeneity at scale n
is ignored at the larger scale, ‘scale n-1’, scale n RVE can be regarded as homogeneous 
mastic in the analysis performed at scale n-1. A group of RVEs are then analyzed using the 
direct-lattice modeling described in Chapter 5. The lattice analysis takes the surface energies 
and modulus of the binder and aggregate and calculates the effective modulus and surface 
energies of RVEs at scale n. This is accomplished by simulating the SEN test for surface 
energies and the uniaxial test for the effective modulus of RVEs. The resulting modulus and 
surface energies from all the RVEs are statistically averaged to eliminate the specimen-tospecimen variation due to the random nature of microstructure. The processed mastic 
properties are, in turn, used for the larger scale (scale n-1) lattice modeling. This procedure 
is recursively applied until all sub-regions of the gradation are considered. In particular, the 

56
microstructure of the specimen being simulated is created and analyzed at scale 1 to predict 
its mechanical response and, eventually, the mixture’s cracking performance.

9. SUMMARY AND FUTURE RESEARCH
In this study, a practical lattice modeling procedure for the characterization of cracking in 
HMA is presented. Its three major components, preprocessor for lattice mesh generation, 
numerical solver coupled with a surface energy-based failure criterion, and a comprehensive 
postprocessor are systematically developed and tested. However, due to the disparate length 
scales associated with microcracking and specimen size, such direct-lattice modeling would 
demand an unrealistic computational cost for modeling even laboratory-scale HMA 
specimens. In order to make the procedure more practical, the multiscale approach is 
implemented to consider the effect of different-sized aggregates at different length scales. 
Such an approach reduces the computational cost significantly, while capturing the 
mechanical phenomena at various length scales. The basic idea of multiscale modeling is to 
model the effects of different sized aggregates at different length scales. Thus, the directlattice modeling is replaced by a series of lattice simulations performed at different length 
scales. The effectiveness of the resulting procedure is illustrated with the help of the 
quantitative prediction of the load-displacement curve from the IDT testing, using a fourscale approach. The proposed virtual testing procedure yields a realistic crack pattern and 
also adequately predicts the load-displacement behavior before reaching peak load. The 
strain fields measured from the DIC during the IDT testing verify the reasonableness of the 
strain calculation in the lattice simulation. 
However, before the procedure can be used with confidence, there are several aspects that 
need to be investigated further. These include (a) replacing the truss elements with beam 

81
elements to simulate variable Poisson’s ratio, (b) developing and evaluating the viscoelastic 
fracture law, and (c) modeling of viscoelastic fracture. These three issues are subjects of 
future research. In addition, since the proposed procedure approximates the material behavior 
in a statistical sense, it is necessary to perform a number of realizations to provide a reliable 
and more accurate prediction. In that case, a parallel computing technique aids significantly 
in reducing the analysis time. Eventually, it is expected that, in its final form, the proposed 
lattice modeling procedure could be used to estimate the cracking performance of HMA 
starting from the component materials’ moduli and surface energies, thus leading to an 
enhanced performance-related mixture specification and mix design optimization.

APPENDIX E - PSEUDO CODE OF POSTPROCESSOR
The graphical postprocessor developed in lattice approach to effectively view and analyze the 
numerical results is written in the environment of Visual Basic 6.0. Its pseudo code is given 
in the following, 
1. Open the data file that contains the original geometry information of the lattice 
network which represents the microstructure of the specimen being simulated. The 
geometry information includes the node coordinates and link information (link type 
node numbers of the near and far end nodes of lattice link) 
2. Calculate the specimen size. 
3. Calculate the scale factor for mesh display, which is defined as the ratio of the left 
graphical window size (Fig. 5.4) and the largest dimension of the specimen. Such 
scale factor is used to multiply the node coordinates so that the lattice network can fit 
the left graphical window. 
4. Starting from the first one in the network, draw each and every lattice link 
successively in the left graphical window. Different types of lattice links are 
represented with different colors. 
5. Now, one can display the original lattice network on screen and virtually check its 
geometry. 
6. Next, read the data file that records the cracking process. Basically, such data file 
contains the following information for each failed link: 

102
Step number – when this link is removed. 
Link near and far end nodes’ numbers – which link is removed. 
Link type – what type of link is removed. 
7. The progressive cracking process is animated by scrolling the horizontal scroll bar 
located under the menu (Fig. 5.4). 
First, read the value of this horizontal scroll bar. This integrate number is used as the 
current time step number in the numerical analysis. 
Then, all the links that were removed before this step are redrew with different color 
and overlapped on the original lattice mesh to virtually represent the cracking trace. 
8. To display the load-deflection curve simultaneously, 
First, read the data file that contains the information needed to construct a loaddeflection curve, i.e., the load applied on the specimen and the corresponding 
deformation measured on the interested location for each and every time step 
sequentially. 
Then, find the maximum values of the load and deflection. 
Two scale factors are then calculated and applied to the load and deflection data 
respectively, to make the load-deflection curve fit the right graphical window (Fig. 
5.4) as what we did in displaying lattice mesh in the left graphical window. 
All the data points (i.e., the points with values of the load applied as vertical 
coordinates and the values of corresponding displacement as horizontal coordinates) 

103
measured before the current time step are linked to each other with straight lines and 
plotted on the right graphical window 
9. Clear the graphical window for the new cases.
